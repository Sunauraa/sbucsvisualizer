Study notes

This module goes from plain traversals to self-balancing multiway trees.

Recursive operations show their call stack next to the tree. Every rotation, split, transfer and fusion gets its own frame, so you can step through each one.

The four rotation cases

After an insert, exactly one node is the lowest unbalanced one. Look at the two steps down from it toward the new key. Their direction picks the rotation:

Casebf(node)bf(child)Fix
Left-Left+2≥ 0single right rotation
Left-Right+2< 0left on child, then right
Right-Right−2≤ 0single left rotation
Right-Left−2> 0right on child, then left

A rotation is a local pointer swap: O(1) time and space. It never changes the in-order sequence, so the BST property survives.

Insert needs at most one rotation (a double counts as one). Delete may need one at every level.

Overflow, underflow, and why heights match

Multiway trees never rotate. Instead of moving nodes around, they change how many keys a node holds. That keeps every leaf at the same depth:

overflow
too many keys after an insert → split, middle key goes up
underflow
too few keys after a delete → transfer from a sibling that has spare keys
no spare keys
→ fusion: merge two siblings plus the separator into one node
grows taller
only when the root splits, so all leaves rise together
grows shorter
only when a fusion empties the root

A (2,4)-tree's height sits between log₄n and log₂n.

A B-tree of order m has height ≤ log⌈m/2⌉n. With m = 100, a million keys fit in 3 levels.

A (2,4)-tree and a red-black tree are the same structure. Colour a 3-node's extra key red and you turn one into the other.

AVL trees stay more tightly balanced, so lookups are faster. Red-black and (2,4) trees do less work per update, so writes are faster.
