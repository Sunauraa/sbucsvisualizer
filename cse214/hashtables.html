Study notes

A hash function turns a key into an index. If two keys never landed on the same index, you'd get O(1) access.

They do land together, though. So the whole subject comes down to handling collisions.

Chaining slows down gently (1 + α/2). Linear probing doesn't: ½(1 + 1/(1−α)²) blows up as α → 1.

That's why open-addressed tables rehash at α ≈ 0.5–0.7, while chaining tables can wait until α ≈ 1.

Things that bite people

composite m
clusters keys; use a prime, or a power of two with a well-mixed hash
deleting by blanking
breaks probe chains; you must use tombstones
tombstone build-up
searches slow down even though n is small; rehash to clear them
bad hash
e.g. summing characters: "cat" and "act" collide; multiply by 31 instead
mutable keys
change a key after inserting it and you can't find it again, because its hash moved
rehash cost
O(n + m) time and O(n + m) space, since every index depends on m

Try this. Switch to linear probing and press random keys a few times. Delete two or three keys, then search for a key that isn't there.
