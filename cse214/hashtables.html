Hash Tables

A hash function turns a key into an index, which would give O(1) access if two keys never landed on the same index. They do — so the whole subject is really about handling collisions.

Chaining degrades gracefully (1 + α/2). Linear probing does not: ½(1 + 1/(1−α)²) blows up as α → 1. That is why open-addressed tables rehash at α ≈ 0.5–0.7 while chaining tables can wait until α ≈ 1.

Things that bite people

composite m
clusters keys; use a prime, or a power of two with a well-mixed hash
deleting by blanking
breaks probe chains — you must use tombstones
tombstone build-up
searches slow down even though n is small; rehash to clear them
bad hash
e.g. summing characters — "cat" and "act" collide; multiply by 31 instead
mutable keys
change a key after inserting and it is unfindable, since its hash moved
rehash cost
Θ(n) and unavoidable — every index depends on m

Try this: switch to linear probing, press random keys a few times, then delete two or three keys and search for something that is not there. You can watch the search walk straight through the tombstones.
